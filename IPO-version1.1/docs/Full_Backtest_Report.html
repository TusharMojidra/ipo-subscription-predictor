Glossary — What Do These Terms Mean?
Before we dive into the numbers, here's a quick guide to every term you'll see in this report:

IPO (Initial Public Offering) — When a private company first sells its shares to the public on the stock exchange. Think of it like a new product launch, but for company ownership.

Listing Gain — The percentage change between the offer price (what you paid during the IPO) and the list price (the first trading price on the exchange). A positive listing gain means the stock opened higher than what you paid — you made money on day one. A negative gain means you lost money.
Example: Offer price = ₹100, List price = ₹115 → Listing Gain = +15% ✅
Example: Offer price = ₹100, List price = ₹85 → Listing Gain = -15% ❌

Subscription Tier (Low / Medium / High) — How much demand there is for an IPO. When more people apply for shares than what's available, the IPO is "oversubscribed." We classify this into 3 buckets:
  • 🔴 Low — Total subscription below 2x. Few people applied. The IPO might list at a loss.
  • 🟡 Medium — Total subscription between 2x and 10x. Moderate interest. Decent chance of gain.
  • 🟢 High — Total subscription above 10x. Everyone wants it! Very likely to list with big gains.
"Total = 25x" means 25 times more shares were applied for than what was available. Very hot IPO!

Tier Accuracy — Out of all IPOs we tested, what percentage did the model correctly classify into the right tier (Low/Medium/High)?
If 100 IPOs were tested and the model got 70 of them right → Tier Accuracy = 70%

Direction Accuracy — Did the model correctly predict whether the stock would go up or down on listing day? This is simpler than predicting the exact gain — it just asks: "Did you get the direction right?"
Actual: +12% (up), Predicted: +8% (up) → Direction ✅ correct
Actual: -5% (down), Predicted: +3% (up) → Direction ❌ wrong

Gain Error (pp) — How far off was the predicted gain from the actual gain, measured in percentage points (pp). A "percentage point" is the simple difference between two percentages.
Actual gain = +5%, Predicted gain = +13% → Error = 8pp (the model was 8 percentage points too high)
Actual gain = -10%, Predicted gain = -3% → Error = 7pp (the model was 7pp too optimistic)

Out-of-Time Split — We trained the model on IPOs from 2010–2023 and tested it on IPOs from 2024–2026. This simulates what would happen in real life — you build the model with past data, then use it to predict the future. If we tested on the same data we trained on, the results would be artificially good (like studying for an exam with the answer key).

QIB / HNI / RII / Total — Different types of investors who apply for an IPO:
  • QIB (Qualified Institutional Buyer) — Big institutions like mutual funds and banks. Their subscription ratio shows how much big money wants in.
  • HNI (High Net-worth Individual) — Wealthy individual investors applying for large amounts (₹2 lakh+).
  • RII (Retail Individual Investor) — Regular people like you and me applying for smaller amounts.
  • Total — The overall subscription ratio across all investor types combined.

Issue Size — Total value of shares being sold in the IPO, measured in ₹ crores. A larger issue size means the company is raising more money. (1 crore = 10 million rupees.)

Offer Price — The price per share set by the company for the IPO. This is what investors pay during the bidding process.

How This Test Works
Think of it like this: We have 648 IPOs of historical data (2010–2026). We split them like an exam:

📚 Training set (438 IPOs, years 2010–2023) — The model studied these IPOs to learn patterns. It saw their issue sizes, prices, subscription numbers, and listing gains. It learned things like "IPOs with high QIB subscription tend to list higher."

🧪 Test set (210 IPOs, years 2024–2026) — The model has never seen these IPOs before. We feed it the inputs (issue size, price, year, subscription numbers) and ask it to predict. Then we compare its predictions with what actually happened. This is the real measure of whether the model works.

Why? Because in real life, you'll use this model to predict future IPOs that haven't happened yet. If the model only works on data it has already seen, it's useless.

Key finding: The model is great at detecting High-tier IPOs (86.2%) but struggles with Low (31%) and Medium (45%). Why?
  • High-tier IPOs are the most common in our dataset (130 out of 210). The model has seen lots of examples.
  • Low-tier IPOs are rare (only 29). With fewer examples to learn from, the model can't distinguish them well.
  • The tier classifier only uses 3 features (issue size, price, year) — it doesn't know the actual subscription numbers. So it's essentially guessing based on "what kind of IPO typically gets low subscription?"

Key Insights & Analysis

🟢 What's Working Well

Direction accuracy is 76.7% — the model correctly predicts whether an IPO will list above or below its offer price about 3 out of 4 times. This is genuinely useful for screening IPOs before applying.

High-tier detection is strong (86.2%) — when the model says "this IPO will have high demand," it's right 86% of the time. This is the most reliable prediction and the most actionable for investors.

75.7% of gain predictions fall within ±20pp — while not precise enough for day trading, this gives investors a rough sense of whether an IPO will list with a gain or a loss.

🔴 What Needs Improvement

Low-tier detection is poor (31%) — the model struggles to identify IPOs that will have very low subscription. It often misclassifies them as Medium. With only 29 Low-tier IPOs in the test set, the model doesn't have enough examples to learn this well.

2026 direction accuracy dropped to 52% — essentially a coin flip. The market conditions in 2026 may be fundamentally different from the training period (2010–2023), causing what data scientists call "model drift."

Max error of 136.33pp — extreme outliers where the model was very wrong. These are rare but costly. They happen when an IPO's subscription looks normal but something unusual causes a huge listing move.

📐 Understanding the Two Models

Our system actually uses two separate models:

1. Tier Classifier (Logistic Regression) — Uses only 3 features: issue size, offer price, and year. It predicts the subscription tier before bidding happens. Think of it as: "Based on the company's size and price, what tier is it likely to fall into?"

2. Gain Predictor (Huber Regression) — Uses 7 features including the actual subscription numbers (QIB, HNI, RII, Total). It predicts the exact listing gain percentage. This one is more accurate because it has more information — but it only works after bidding closes and subscription data is available.

This two-model approach mirrors real life: before an IPO opens, you only know the issue size and price. After bidding closes, you also know the subscription ratios. The first model helps you decide whether to apply; the second helps you estimate how much you might gain.
